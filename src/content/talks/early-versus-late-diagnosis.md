### Introduction

For many neurodivergent people, receiving a diagnosis brings a significant shift in perspective. A lifetime of experiences (of struggling, of being told to try harder, of feeling out of step with the people around them) suddenly has a framework.

But that moment of clarity doesn’t arrive at the same point in life for everyone. One of the biggest factors shaping the diagnosis experience is when it happens. Today I want to explore what it means to be diagnosed early in life versus later, and what that difference can mean for the individual, for their sense of identity, and for the support available to them.

### What we mean

First, a quick note on what we mean by neurodiversity. We’re talking about a wide range of conditions: autism, ADHD, dyslexia, dyscalculia, dyspraxia, Tourette’s, and others. Each one is different, and every individual’s experience is different too. But they share something in common: they’re all ways of thinking, processing, and experiencing the world that diverge from what’s considered “typical”.

There’s no single agreed definition of what counts as “early” or “late” diagnosis. Broadly, early diagnosis tends to mean being identified in childhood, sometimes in the first few years of school, or even before. Late diagnosis typically means reaching adulthood, sometimes middle age or beyond, without ever having a formal assessment.

The gap between those two experiences is significant. Increasingly, we’re hearing from people diagnosed in their 30s, 40s, 50s, and later, who are re-examining what that means for how they understand themselves and their past.

### Early diagnosis

Being diagnosed early (especially in childhood) can open doors. With the right support, early diagnosis means children can access tailored teaching approaches, extra time in exams, speech and language therapy, occupational therapy, or specialist school provision. It can mean parents and teachers understand a child’s needs before frustration hardens into low self-esteem.

Early diagnosis can also be protective. When a child understands why they experience the world differently, they’re better equipped to advocate for themselves. They grow up knowing their brain works differently, not that they’re broken.

But early diagnosis isn’t uniformly positive. Some people diagnosed as children describe being defined entirely by their diagnosis, having their potential underestimated, being pulled out of mainstream settings unnecessarily, or being reduced to a label rather than seen as a whole person. There’s also the question of which children get diagnosed early. Historically, diagnostic criteria were built around a narrow set of presentations, most commonly young white boys. Girls, children of colour, and children from lower-income families have all been significantly under-diagnosed, even when they were struggling just as much.

So early diagnosis can be a lifeline, but access to it, and the quality of support that follows, is deeply unequal.

### Late diagnosis

For people diagnosed as adults, the path to that point has often involved decades of coping strategies, masking, and self-doubt. Masking is the process of consciously or unconsciously suppressing neurodivergent traits to fit in, mirroring others’ social behaviour, rehearsing conversations, working to appear “normal”. Many late-diagnosed people describe getting through life by working significantly harder for the same results, without understanding why.

The diagnosis itself can bring considerable relief: a framework that makes sense of a lifetime of experiences. But it’s often followed by something more complicated: grief. Grief for the years spent struggling without support, for the professional or personal setbacks that might have been different, for the child they once were who deserved help and didn’t receive it.

Late diagnosis is particularly common, and particularly complex, for women and non-binary people with ADHD and autism, whose presentations often don’t match the textbook descriptions. It’s also common for people with dyslexia or dyscalculia who simply found ways to compensate, sometimes so successfully that no one ever thought to look.

Late diagnosis also raises a practical challenge: adult services are patchier than children’s services. There’s less specialist support available once you’re over 18. The waiting lists in the NHS are long. And there’s a real tension between finally having language for your experience, and realising the system wasn’t really built to support you at this stage of life.

### Diagnosis and identity

One of the most profound effects of when you’re diagnosed is how it shapes your identity.

Someone diagnosed at six years old grows up incorporating neurodivergence into their sense of self from the start; it’s part of who they are.

Someone diagnosed at 45 has spent decades constructing an identity without that framework, often developing self-critical explanations for every difficulty they faced: being “lazy”, “not clever enough”, “too distracted”. A late diagnosis can mean revisiting those explanations and reinterpreting them, a process that can be both liberating and disorienting.

It also raises interesting questions about community. Some late-diagnosed people feel strongly connected to the neurodivergent community and find real solidarity there. Others feel uncertain about claiming an identity they only discovered recently. There’s no right answer, but it’s worth being aware that neurodivergent identity is something people arrive at on very different timelines.

### Why it matters

Whether you’re neurodivergent yourself, or here as an ally, understanding this timeline matters.

If someone shares a late diagnosis with you, recognise that they may be processing a lot: not just new information, but a recontextualisation of their whole life. Curiosity and openness are more useful than assumptions.

If you work with or manage people, it’s worth knowing that some of your colleagues may have diagnoses, early or late, that shape how they work best. And some may be undiagnosed entirely, still searching for an explanation for experiences they can’t yet name.

Creating environments where neurodivergent people feel safe to be open about their experiences, without fear of being reduced to a label or having their abilities questioned, matters for everyone on that spectrum of experience.

### Closing

Early versus late diagnosis is a deeply personal topic, and many of you will have your own experiences to bring to this, whether you’ve been through a diagnosis yourself, supported someone through one, or are still somewhere in that process.

What I hope comes through is a sense of the complexity involved. Diagnosis can be enormously valuable, but it isn’t a simple or equally accessible experience, and the timing shapes it in ways that run through a person’s entire life.

As we move into discussion, I’d welcome whatever resonates with you, whatever surprises you, or whatever you’d add. And if you’d like to share your own experience (early, late, or still figuring it out) this is the right space for it.
