### Slide 4

The heading “Semantics” on a yellow background. This is the first of 7 slides with the same layout:
the heading in the top left, a short “What is” description under it, “Who does this help?” in the top
right, and a list under “Information”.

- “What are semantics?” “What code actually means”
- “Who does this help?” “Absolutely everyone”

The second point in the list mentions search engine optimisation (SEO). Under “Information”:

- “Using correct semantics adds an incredible amount of information, which can then be used by
  screen readers and web scrapers”
- “For instance, using a section element rather than a div element gives more meaning to your code.
  A web scraper can read our site better, improving our SEO.”
- “It saves us work in the long run, because we’re using the extra features that are built in to
  each element, instead of having to build that functionality ourselves.”

### Slide 5

The heading “Alt text” on a pink background.

- “What is alt text?” “Short, descriptive text to describe an image.”
- “Who does this help?” “Anyone with visual impairments, and those with slow internet”

Under “Information”:

- “It shouldn’t start with ‘An image of…’ or ‘A picture of…’”
- “For example, an image with alt text of ‘A black cat sat on a brown sofa’ will be read by a screen
  reader as ‘Image. A black cat sat on a brown sofa’.”
- “Alt text should be used when an image is important to the meaning of the page. Decorative images
  don’t need alt text, i.e. Doodles. Instead, alt text should be set as blank so that a screen
  reader will skip past it.”

### Slide 6

The heading “Tap Targets” on a green background.

- “What are tap targets?” “Areas of a page that can be interacted with”
- “Who does this help?” “Anyone with motor skills impairments, including the elderly”

Under “Information”:

- “Each tap target should be a minimum of 44px height and 44px width. However, the giffgaff standard
  is 48px height and 48px width.”
- “Where two tap targets are next to each other, there should be a minimum of an 8px gap between the
  two. However, the giffgaff standard is 16px”

### Slide 7

The heading “Colour Contrast” on a yellow background.

- “What is colour contrast?” “Ensuring that text colour and background colour are different enough
  that the text is readable”
- “Who does this help?” “Everyone, but specifically anyone with colour vision deficiency, and those
  with sun glare on their screens”

Under “Information”:

- “How well one colour stands out from another determines whether people will be able to read the
  information”
- “The giffgaff colour palette has been designed to have sufficient colour contrast”
- “Search for ‘colour contrast checker’ for an easy tool to check that your colours are contrasting”
- “Use the giffgaff colour palette with the text colours as shown”

In the bottom right is a small picture of 6 colours from the giffgaff colour palette. Each one is a

- “olive”, in black text: “#B9CC3F rgb(185, 204, 63)”
- “turquoise”, in black text: “#00CEB3 rgb(0, 206, 179)”

### Slide 8

The heading “Link text” on a pink background.

- “What is link text?” “The text that is clickable for links and buttons”
- “Who does this help?” “Anyone with visual impairments, including the elderly”

Under “Information”:

- “Every link or button should describe where the link or button will take you, in the clickable
  text”
- “Screen readers have the ability to jump from one link to the next, skipping all non-link text”
- “If all your links are ‘Click here’ or ‘Read more’, then that gives no information to the user
  about what that link will do”
- “For instance, instead of ‘Click here for a blog article’, use ‘A blog article’”

The words “Click here”, “Read more” and “A blog article” are underlined, to show which words would be
the clickable link text. They’re examples, not real links.

### Slide 9

The heading “Reading Age” on a green background.

- “What is reading age?” “The reading level of the average person.”
- “Who does this help?” “Everyone, especially those with cognitive impairments”

Under “Information”:

- “In the UK, the average reading age is 9 to 11 years old. This means that the average person reads
  at the same level as a 9 to 11 year old.”
- “This is not limited to the UK, in the USA the average reading age is 9 years old.”
- “At giffgaff, we aim to write for a reading age of 9, avoiding long and complicated words or
  sentences.”

### Slide 10

The heading “Pseudo-classes” on a yellow background.

- “What are pseudo-classes?” “They define a special state of an element”
- “Who does this help?” “Everyone, especially those who use keyboards”

Under “Information”:

- “These help to convey information”
- “Using a hover pseudo-class shows what a user has hovered their mouse over”
- “For keyboard-only users, they can’t benefit from hover states. Instead, focus can be used with the
  same styling as hover to convey the same information: this is where your cursor is”
- “This is not just relevant for the web, as the app can be used on a tablet, with a keyboard and
  mouse attached”
